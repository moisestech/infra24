## Concept

**If code can become the architecture of a cultural institution, who gets to redesign it?**

Traditional cultural institutions often organize around buildings, staff, boards, collections, and departments, with software serving as supporting infrastructure. DCC Miami tests a different proposition: that a digitally native, artist-led institution can be organized through its own evolving code, reciprocal human relationships, distributed maker capacity, and transparent protocols.

This does not render physical institutions obsolete. Creative networks still depend on studios, production spaces, skilled labor, gathering places, and trust. The artistic opportunity is to make the often-invisible *relational architecture* of an institution perceptible, collectively editable, and eventually material.

**The Network Makes Itself** extends DCC's existing Community Atlas into an interactive web artwork. Instead of automatically representing people as data points, participants become authors who choose what their node means and what relationships they consent to make public. Updating, declining, or reconfiguring a node transforms the composition.

At a collectively agreed moment, participants freeze one state of the work and translate its geometry into a small fabricated sculptural edition, making the infrastructure of a network into a material object.

## Why this is an artwork

The interface is not a database visualization or an advertising tool. The artwork's primary gesture is that participants can negotiate the organizational image of the very community they inhabit. Its formal choices ask questions about cultural labor, algorithmic classification, representation, trust, affiliation, and whose intentions survive when humans become machine-readable.

The project also asks whether a network's architecture can be made legible without claiming ownership of the people who sustain it. Some relationships should not be displayed. Some participants will choose absence, anonymity, or refusal. Those decisions must be part of the artwork's form, not treated as missing data.

## Participation: a four-part score

1. **AUTHOR:** Opt-in participants define or revise a node through their practice, a current SHOW / NEED / OFFER, and the choice to be public or not.
2. **CONNECT:** A shared declared interest, a proposed connection, and a mutually approved real collaboration have visibly different states. Public person-to-person edges require the consent of both parties.
3. **TRANSFORM:** Each approved change alters the composition, so the web interface itself is the time-based, collectively authored artwork.
4. **MATERIALIZE:** Participants choose one consent-cleared snapshot to fabricate as a physical study. A deterministic vector export becomes an input to maker-reviewed prototyping, not automatically machine-ready cut instructions.

## Pilot and grant support

A microgrant-sized experiment could invite **eight opt-in artists**, compensating each for bounded participation and feedback rather than asking an entire network to volunteer for an open-ended build. The first completed artwork would consist of an interactive public study, the agreed digital edition, one small fabricated edition, and documentation of the aesthetic, technical, and consent decisions.

## Ethics / governance

A member's presence in DCC, WhatsApp, an internal roster, or a public event does not authorize inclusion in the artwork. Network membership, public profile visibility, public relationship approval, and lasting physical-edition inclusion are separate permissions.

Machine-inferred affinity is never presented as an agreed collaboration. Participants can revise or remove digital contributions. Since fabricated objects cannot be instantly altered or withdrawn, the group must approve edition-specific permissions and discuss how errors, credits, and withdrawals are handled before fabrication.

The long-term institutional question is whether stewardship can be transferred to participants—not whether a founder can automate more correspondence.

## Continuing research

The project extends *Born Into the Machine*, an artistic inquiry into what happens when computational systems stop being discrete tools and become the climate in which identity, labor, and social life are organized.

It also builds on DCC's ongoing research into institutional capacity, opt-in representation, human-scale consent, and machine-readable communication. These remain internal working documents; the public proposal stands independently of private community records.
