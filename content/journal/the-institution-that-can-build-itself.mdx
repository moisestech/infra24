# The Institution That Can Build Itself

*On digitally native institutions, self-organizing communities, and the infrastructure of cultural agency.*

To start a creative community today, you are often expected to become a customer before you become an institution.

You need a website, so you subscribe to one. You need to invite people, so you open another account. Ticketing, newsletters, member records, donations, payments, and project management each introduce another interface and another recurring expense. Gradually, the organization acquires a collection of systems that may barely speak to one another.

None of these products is inherently the problem. Good software can save small teams from reinventing complicated, high-risk work. The problem is when purchasing software becomes a substitute for developing the capacity to understand and change the systems on which the community depends.

An institution can own a domain, pay its invoices, and still have almost no control over how its digital life is organized.

## Two futures, neither complete

I see two possible responses.

In the first, the cultural institution becomes digitally native. Technical judgment moves into leadership. The organization can evaluate which tools to buy, which systems to build, which information to preserve, and which dependencies it can afford. It treats software as part of the cultural program, not merely an administrative utility.

In the second, communities gain enough shared technical and organizational capacity that they need less of the traditional institution in the first place. People coordinate through networks of studios, operators, educators, and artists. They share knowledge and access without requiring every collaboration to pass through a centralized organization.

Both futures appeal to me. Both have problems.

A digitally sophisticated institution can still become hierarchical, exclusionary, or overly attached to its own infrastructure. A decentralized community can become dependent on invisible volunteer labor, informal gatekeepers, or one person who knows how everything works. Neither organizational form guarantees access or accountability.

The more useful question may not be which model wins. It may be what each can learn from the other.

## Technical literacy is a cultural responsibility

The systems an organization uses do more than store information. They decide which activities can be recorded, which relationships become visible, who can participate, and who is allowed to change the rules.

A membership database is also a model of belonging. An intake form is also a theory of what counts as a valid request. A booking workflow can quietly determine which artists get access to an expensive machine.

Technical decisions are therefore cultural decisions.

That doesn't mean every director needs to program, or that a community should build its own ticketing service. It means leadership should understand the consequences of the infrastructure it chooses. Who owns the data? Can the system be migrated? What happens when a vendor changes its prices? Who maintains the custom code? Can members question the rules?

The distinction is between outsourcing an implementation and outsourcing judgment.

## AI changes the threshold, not the obligation

AI-assisted development makes it easier to prototype interfaces and workflows. Open-source software can be adapted more quickly. A small team can attempt projects that recently required far more specialized labor.

But generating code is not the same as sustaining infrastructure.

Security, accessibility, documentation, backups, maintenance, privacy, and human support do not disappear. A custom application that only its founder understands is not communal capacity. It is another dependency, even if no subscription appears on the credit-card statement.

So the relevant measure is not how much software a community builds. It is how much agency the community gains without quietly transferring costs to unpaid technical people.

Sometimes the right answer will be a commercial subscription. Sometimes an open-source tool. Sometimes a shared service maintained with partner organizations. Sometimes a small custom application. What matters is whether those decisions remain intelligible, reversible, and accountable.

## Building a network before a building

This question sits underneath DCC Miami.

Miami already has artists, machines, studios, educators, fabricators, and technical specialists. A practical challenge is that these resources often remain difficult to navigate together. The person with an idea may not know who can prepare the file, quote the project, fabricate a part, finish the work, or install it. Meanwhile, another person may have the equipment and expertise but no dependable way for requests to reach them.

We're testing whether a shared operating layer can make these existing capabilities usable as a network.

The work is both social and technical: introductions, consent, scoping, paid labor, scheduling, records, documentation, and ways for people to return after the first interaction. A software system may help coordinate that work, but it cannot substitute for trust.

Studio 43 is one node, not the whole institution. The larger question is whether knowledge and opportunity can move through the network without requiring the founder to personally carry every handoff.

That is a hypothesis to test through real projects, not a result we can claim simply because the platform exists.

## An institution as a capacity

Maybe the most interesting future is neither a traditional institution that purchases increasingly sophisticated software nor a supposedly autonomous network that refuses institutional responsibility.

Maybe it is a community capable of developing institutional functions when it needs them: governance, memory, coordination, production, public programs, care, and durable economic relationships.

Some of these functions might be centralized; others distributed. Some might require paid staff; others shared protocols. The organization becomes less important as a container and more important as a capacity that people can sustain together.

The question I want DCC Miami to keep asking is simple: after someone participates, does the network become more useful to the next person?

Not only more visible. Not only larger. More capable.

If the answer can eventually be demonstrated, perhaps we will have built something more consequential than another cultural platform: a community that can participate in designing, maintaining, and changing the infrastructure of its own creative life.
