In a larger story about creative infrastructure, there is a temptation to begin with a map.

Every studio becomes a dot. Every machine becomes an icon. Every talented person becomes a capability. A network starts to look complete before anyone has attempted to work through it.

I want to begin somewhere smaller: Studio 43 at Bakehouse Art Complex in Miami.

This is the physical starting point for one DCC experiment. It is not a claim that we have built a citywide institution. It is a place where the gap between an idea and the labor required to realize it becomes difficult to ignore.

## A room is useful because it has limits

Studio 43 is roughly twelve by twenty-four feet. It can hold a small group of people, a conversation, a tabletop demonstration, some prototypes, and equipment. It cannot hold all of Miami's digital culture.

That constraint is part of the lesson.

A studio may be able to host a small class, review a file, test a 3D print, or help someone identify what a project needs next. But another project might require machining, electronics knowledge, larger production equipment, finishing space, installation help, or a specialist who works elsewhere.

When someone arrives with a question the room cannot answer, there are two possibilities.

We can treat the lack of equipment as the end of access. Or we can treat it as the start of a network question: who has the capability, under what terms, and how can we make the introduction useful?

The second answer is what DCC is trying to learn.

## Not every idea arrives as a file

The public phrase *Print My File* is useful because it describes something concrete.

But a surprising amount of the meaningful work may happen before a file is ready to print.

What material does the object need? How much finish is expected? Is the geometry usable? Does the artist need a technician, a class, or a producer? Is there a budget? Is the deadline realistic? Does the requested process even make sense?

Someone has to translate an artistic intention into a sequence of decisions, with the artist rather than over them.

That translation has a cost, and it takes skill. Treating it as free preliminary labor is one reason creative-technical practices struggle to remain sustainable.

At Node 001, we want to make those decisions more visible: what was asked, what was proposed, who does the work, how it is compensated, and what happens when the work is complete.

## The operator is not invisible

The machinery may be photogenic. The labor around it rarely is.

Files need inspection. Machines need calibration. Materials can fail. Finishing takes time. A print that looks promising on screen may require an entirely different production strategy.

If a network routes more work to specialists but doesn't clarify scope, safety, payment, or ownership, it has not necessarily improved anyone's life.

DCC's production pilot is designed around a modest and demanding threshold: ten paid jobs, verified operators, at least two working nodes, an external-capacity job, clear quotes before work, and documented closeout. These are evaluation goals, not results already achieved.

We will also need to notice what the metrics don't say. Was the job worth taking? Did the artist understand the process? Could someone else repeat the handoff? Did a member gain useful confidence even when no transaction occurred?

Capacity is not merely the number of printers a network can list.

It is the reliability of the relationships and agreements that make those printers useful.

## A studio can be a node without becoming the center

I am wary of calling every space a hub. The word can make distributed communities sound as though they still need one central gatekeeper.

A node should be useful because it performs some functions well and connects people to the rest of the system. It should not need to be the place where every relationship is approved or every project is produced.

Studio 43 can host the first tests. It can collect practical lessons, demonstrate a process, and reveal the questions our software and protocols have not yet anticipated.

But the infrastructure gets interesting only when another artist or operator can help someone without requiring me to personally manage every step.

That is the work of turning a studio into a node: not announcing a network, but making one handoff clearer, one scope fairer, one collaboration easier to repeat.

## Notes from an unfinished experiment

This field note is a starting condition, not a victory report.

There are still workflows to verify, people to onboard, costs to understand, and agreements to write. A community also needs room for exchange that is not immediately converted into a job or a lead.

I hope we can publish those lessons as we encounter them—including when a proposed workflow fails.

What matters about this room is not its square footage or the collection of equipment inside it.

It is the possibility that what we learn here can help people make work somewhere else.

Node 001 is where that question becomes material.
