There is a peculiar moment in starting a cultural initiative when almost every organizational question appears to have a software answer.

Where will people find us? A website builder.

How will we invite them? An event platform.

Where will the conversation happen? A group chat.

How will we collect payments? A checkout page.

How will we remember who participated? A database.

I use these systems myself. They can be excellent. The problem isn't that cultural communities buy software. The problem is the assumption that assembling enough services is equivalent to building an organization.

A subscription can provision a feature. It cannot tell you what the feature should mean to the people who use it.

## The stack is not the culture

Most software is sold around an administrative task. Send a newsletter. Sell a ticket. Create a form. Book a room. Coordinate a team.

Cultural work rarely stays inside those categories.

Someone attends a workshop and returns three months later with a project. Someone else offers to help with a difficult material. A studio visit becomes a collaboration. A conversation introduces an artist to a fabricator who can actually finish the piece. A friend brings a friend.

The activity moves between learning, trust, production, friendship, and paid work. Each platform records only the slice it was designed to recognize.

You can end up with five accounts and no shared understanding of what happened.

This is more than inconvenient administration. If the organization only sees registrations, clicks, and transactions, it may begin to confuse those records with the life of the community itself.

Participation is not the same as attendance. Belonging is not the same as membership. A useful introduction may never become a sale.

## The hidden cost of convenient defaults

Software comes with assumptions.

An event page assumes there is an event and a registrant. A CRM assumes there is a contact and a pipeline. A messaging platform assumes people want to communicate in a feed. Those assumptions are reasonable within each product; they are not neutral when combined into the operating model of a community.

Who counts as a member? Can someone ask for help without becoming a sales lead? Who can view an artist's skills? Does a public Instagram profile imply consent to be added to a directory? What happens when someone leaves?

These are cultural and governance decisions. The software should implement them, not silently make them.

A useful technical question is therefore not just *Can this platform do it?* but *Whose rules are we adopting when we use it?*

## Buying, building, and borrowing

I don't think every cultural organization should replace commercial services with custom applications. That would be an expensive mistake.

Buying is often the right choice for payments, security-sensitive workflows, accessibility features, and mature administrative tasks. Open-source tools may offer flexibility but still require hosting and maintenance. Custom code can clarify a very specific workflow while creating another technical dependency.

AI-assisted development changes how quickly we can prototype. It does not eliminate the responsibilities that come after the prototype: testing, documentation, privacy, operating costs, migrations, support, and succession.

The useful distinction isn't proprietary versus open source or purchased versus built.

It is between a tool we understand well enough to govern and a tool whose defaults we have mistaken for our own values.

If an application disappears tomorrow, what survives? Our data? The relationships? The way of working? The knowledge to rebuild what mattered?

## Why DCC is testing a different approach

DCC Miami begins with an unusually simple observation: people, skills, machines, and cultural organizations already exist throughout the city. It is often difficult to know how to find and work with them together.

We are exploring a shared operating layer that can make that capacity more legible and reachable.

That includes public writing, a place for conversation, ways to describe an actual need, consent-based introductions, scoping, quotes, paid technical labor, and records of what was learned. A physical studio matters. So does the ability to route an appropriate request beyond that studio.

A WhatsApp group is not the network. It is one meeting surface.

A database is not community memory unless it captures information responsibly and makes future action easier. A website is not an institution simply because it contains a mission statement.

The test is whether a person arrives with something they want to learn or make and leaves with more genuine possibility than they had before.

## What cannot be subscribed to

You cannot purchase reciprocity as a software feature.

You cannot automate somebody into trusting a stranger.

You cannot buy the experience of another artist noticing what you're trying to do and offering precisely the missing piece.

You can, however, design systems that make those encounters more likely, easier to sustain, and less dependent on one exhausted organizer.

That is the difference between using software to administer cultural activity and developing the capacity for a culture to organize itself.

We will keep using useful services. We will keep questioning their defaults. We will build only where the work calls for it.

The ambition is not to own more technology.

It is for more of the community to have agency over the infrastructure shaping its creative life.
