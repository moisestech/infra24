There is a moment I keep encountering with artists.

They have an idea.

They can see the work clearly enough to describe it. Maybe they have a sketch, a render, a broken prototype, a folder of reference images, or a 3D model that almost works.

And then the conversation stops at a technical problem.

"I don't know Blender."

"I don't know how to prepare this file for printing."

"I've never worked with electronics."

"I don't know how to install this."

"I don't know what kind of screen I need."

"I don't know how to make the physical part."

The default response to these problems is often educational.

Here is a tutorial.

Here is a course.

Here is a piece of software you should learn.

Here is another account you should create.

Sometimes that is exactly the right answer. Learning new tools is part of being an artist, and technical independence can open enormous possibilities.

But I increasingly think we have confused **access to technology** with **the obligation to personally master every technology involved in making a work.**

Those are not the same thing.

An artist does not need to know everything.

They need to know enough to make decisions, ask better questions, understand what is possible, and recognize when another person's expertise should enter the process.

## The myth of the self-sufficient digital artist

Digital culture arrived with a strange promise.

The computer would collapse entire production systems into one machine.

The artist could write, photograph, edit, animate, publish, code, compose, fabricate, distribute, and document from the same desk.

In many ways, that happened.

The tools became extraordinarily accessible.

But somewhere along the way, accessibility turned into expectation.

Now the artist is often expected to also be:

a designer,

a developer,

a video editor,

a fabricator,

a photographer,

a social media manager,

a producer,

an installer,

an archivist,

a grant writer,

and increasingly, someone who understands AI systems, 3D workflows, electronics, interactive media, and whatever new technical layer arrives next.

We celebrate this as creative independence.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it is just **too many jobs assigned to one person.**

Other mature creative fields already understand this.

A filmmaker is not considered less serious because someone else operates the camera.

An architect does not personally pour the concrete.

A musician can understand sound deeply without manufacturing the microphone.

Artists have always worked through networks of specialized knowledge.

Digital art should not be different.

## Knowing what you don't know is a skill

There is a form of technical confidence that has very little to do with knowing every piece of software.

It sounds more like:

"I don't know how to do this yet, but I know what kind of person I need."

That is a sophisticated skill.

It requires enough literacy to distinguish between a design problem, a fabrication problem, a software problem, an installation problem, and a production problem.

It means understanding enough about the material or technology to participate in decisions without pretending to be the expert in every step.

That distinction matters.

Because when every unfamiliar problem becomes another course the artist is expected to take, the work slows down.

Sometimes dramatically.

A sculpture that needs one hour of CAD repair can become a three-week detour into learning CAD.

An interactive installation that needs a developer can become six months of an artist trying to teach themselves programming.

A file that needs to be prepared for fabrication becomes an entire education in manufacturing.

There is nothing wrong with learning those things.

But we should ask a more useful question first:

**Does the artist need to learn this skill, or does the project need access to this skill?**

Those are very different problems.

## Expertise is infrastructure

This is one of the ideas we keep returning to at DCC Miami.

When we talk about infrastructure, it is easy to picture equipment.

Printers.

Screens.

Computers.

Headsets.

Tools.

But expertise is infrastructure too.

A person who knows why a print keeps failing is infrastructure.

Someone who can repair a mesh is infrastructure.

Someone who understands AV installation is infrastructure.

Someone who can translate an artist's visual intention into a fabrication process is infrastructure.

Someone who knows which material not to use is infrastructure.

Someone who has already made the mistake you are about to make is infrastructure.

These capabilities often exist in a cultural ecosystem, but they exist **in people rather than buildings**.

And people are much harder to inventory than machines.

You can publish an equipment list.

It is harder to publish:

Who knows what?

Who is good at explaining it?

Who likes working with artists?

Who can fabricate?

Who can teach?

Who can troubleshoot?

Who wants to collaborate?

Who should be paid for specialized work rather than casually asked for a favor?

That is the beginning of a different kind of infrastructure.

Not merely access to tools.

**Access to capabilities.**

## Collaboration should not mean extraction

There is a danger here too.

Creative communities are often very good at informal exchange.

Someone knows Blender.

Someone has a printer.

Someone knows how to solder.

Someone can fix the website.

Someone has access to a projector.

And suddenly the same people become the unpaid technical support system for everyone around them.

That is not the network I am interested in building.

A healthy network has to distinguish between:

sharing knowledge,

teaching,

collaborating,

consulting,

and doing production work.

Those are different kinds of labor.

Sometimes the right exchange is:

"Can you show me how?"

Sometimes it is:

"Can we make this together?"

And sometimes it is simply:

"What would you charge to do this part of the project?"

That clarity matters.

If we want sustainable cultural infrastructure, expertise cannot remain invisible.

The people maintaining the system need to be recognized as participants in the work, not just the people called when something breaks.

## The role between the artist and the machine

I think there is another role becoming increasingly important in digital culture.

It sits somewhere between producer, technical director, fabricator, creative technologist, and translator.

The job is not necessarily to execute every technical task.

It is to understand the project well enough to help route it.

You want to make this physical?

Here are the fabrication options.

You want this installation to respond to people?

Here is where software enters.

You have a 3D model but it cannot be printed?

Here is the technical problem.

You need a large display?

Here are the installation constraints you should consider before choosing one.

You do not know who can do the next part?

Here are two people who might.

That connective role is extremely valuable because artists often do not need more information.

They need **direction through the information.**

The internet already contains more tutorials than anyone can finish.

What is scarce is judgment.

What applies here?

What does not?

What is worth learning?

What should be delegated?

What is the cheapest experiment?

Who has done this before?

What can we test before spending money?

That kind of guidance can turn an overwhelming technical problem into a series of manageable decisions.

## Sometimes the workshop is the answer

This does not mean education becomes less important.

The opposite.

It means education can become more intentional.

A workshop is useful when understanding the underlying process expands the artist's practice.

Learning fabrication basics might help an artist design better objects.

Learning how an AI-generated 3D model differs from a fabrication-ready model can prevent predictable problems.

Learning enough code to understand interaction might completely change what an artist imagines is possible.

But a workshop should not quietly carry the expectation:

**Now you should be able to do all of this alone.**

Introduction is not mastery.

Exposure is not independence.

And independence should not always be the goal.

A good educational experience can teach someone enough to know:

what they can handle themselves,

what they want to keep learning,

and when to bring in another person.

That is a successful outcome too.

## What we are testing at DCC Miami

One of the things we are trying to understand is how to make these capabilities more visible.

Not just:

What equipment exists?

But:

What can people actually help one another do?

Who understands fabrication?

Who understands file preparation?

Who understands interactive systems?

Who can teach?

Who can install?

Who can document?

Who knows production?

Who wants to collaborate?

The longer-term opportunity is not simply to build a room where artists can access machines.

It is to build a network where an artist can arrive with an unfinished technical question and leave with a believable next step.

Sometimes that step is learning.

Sometimes it is prototyping.

Sometimes it is paying someone.

Sometimes it is being introduced to another artist.

Sometimes it is discovering that the original idea should change.

That last one matters too.

The right collaborator does not simply execute your idea.

They can change what you understand the idea to be.

## The artist still has to decide

None of this removes authorship.

The artist still has to make the difficult decisions.

What matters?

What should the work do?

What can change?

What cannot?

What is the experience?

What is the material saying?

What is worth spending money on?

What compromises destroy the work?

What compromises make it possible?

Technical collaborators can expand the available options.

They cannot answer those questions for you.

That is why I do not think the future artist is someone who knows every tool.

I think the future artist is someone who can move intelligently between **vision, literacy, collaboration, and judgment.**

Someone who is curious enough to learn.

Confident enough to ask.

Specific enough to direct.

And secure enough to admit when another person knows more.

## Access can mean knowing who to call

We often describe access in physical terms.

Access to a studio.

Access to a printer.

Access to software.

Access to a lab.

But one of the most valuable forms of access is much simpler:

**knowing who to call.**

Knowing that there is someone who understands the problem.

Knowing they will take the idea seriously.

Knowing the question is not too basic.

Knowing there is a path from "I have no idea how to make this" to "let's figure out the first test."
