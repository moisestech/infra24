Cultural organizations often talk about technology as if the main decision is which product to buy.

A collections system.

A ticketing stack.

A website rebuild.

A digital strategy consultant.

An AI pilot.

Those purchases are visible. They produce invoices, launch emails, and the feeling of progress.

What stays harder to see is the **institution that has to exist around the purchase**: the people who can decide, the time it takes to decide, the documentation that lets someone else continue the work, and the capacity that actually reaches artists.

That surrounding institution is usually unpaid, unnamed, or collapsed into one technically fluent staff member.

This essay starts from a different question.

**What capacity should a cultural organization be expected to create with the resources it already has?**

Not: what should it buy next.

Not: how impressive can the stack look from the outside.

What can it *run*?

<EditorialPullQuote>
  Digital literacy is becoming an executive competency.
</EditorialPullQuote>

That is a DCC argument, not a settled industry fact.

It means the people who govern a cultural organization increasingly need enough technical judgment to own decisions about systems, vendors, data, automation, and measurement. Not enough to become engineers. Enough to stop outsourcing the institution’s thinking.

## The invisible institution

Every cultural organization already runs on more technology than its public story admits.

Email.

Shared drives.

Passwords in a browser.

A CRM that only one person understands.

A website no one can edit without a ticket.

A grant report assembled by screenshots.

A backup that may or may not exist.

This is the invisible institution. It is not a “digital department.” It is the everyday technical life of the organization, usually maintained by whoever happened to learn the tools.

When that person leaves, the institution discovers what it actually owned: licenses, not capacity.

DCC is trying to make that invisible layer visible enough to govern.

## The technical gap is becoming a leadership gap

For a long time, digital work inside cultural organizations could be treated as a specialist lane.

Someone “does the website.”

Someone “handles social.”

Someone “knows the database.”

That arrangement is breaking down. Not because every director must code, but because technical decisions now shape budget, labor, public programs, artist access, and institutional memory.

If leadership cannot tell the difference between a tool and a dependency, the organization will keep renting judgment.<Cite id="cultural-org-enterprise-tech" />

The claim here is modest, and still a hypothesis:

Organizations that **own their technical decisions** can often create more operational capacity per dollar than organizations that remain **passive clients of enterprise technology**.

We do not yet have DCC’s own ledger to prove the second half of that sentence. The first half is the operating stance.

<EditorialPullQuote>
  DCC does not need to own its technology. It needs to own its technical decisions.
</EditorialPullQuote>

Owning the decision is not the same as building everything in-house.

It is the difference between:

- knowing why a system exists,
- knowing what happens if it fails,
- knowing who can change it,
- and knowing when a vendor is selling a substitute for institutional literacy.

A client of technology waits to be told what is possible.

An owner of institutional capacity can ask whether the proposed system creates more artist-facing work, or only more internal administration.

## Renting technical judgment

Cultural organizations are fluent at renting prestige: consultants, agencies, platforms, “digital transformation” packages.

Some of that labor is necessary. DCC uses vendors too.

The problem is when the rental includes **the judgment itself**.

Then the organization can no longer tell:

what is a real constraint,

what is a vendor default,

what is a one-time setup,

and what will become a permanent operating cost.

Decision latency is part of that cost.

How long does it take to answer a simple technical question?

How many people must be asked?

How often does a project stall because nobody is allowed to decide?

Latency is not a dashboard metric yet. It is a felt operating condition. DCC intends to measure it later. For now it is named so it cannot hide inside “we need a better tool.”

## Humanizing the digital

These questions did not begin with DCC.

Museum technology already has a professional field. The Museum Computer Network exists so that cultural workers can think together about systems, data, and public access — not only about screens.<Cite id="mcn" />

Museums and the Web has spent decades treating the networked museum as a research and practice problem, not a marketing upgrade.<Cite id="museweb" />

“Humanizing the Digital” belongs in that lineage: a reminder that digital infrastructure is a human and institutional problem before it is a product problem.<Cite id="humanizing-the-digital" />

DCC’s contribution is not “institutions should use technology.”

It is closer to:

**What happens when digital literacy is embedded in the formation, governance, economics, and artist-facing operations of a cultural organization, rather than added afterward as a transformation project?**

Digital formation, not only digital transformation.

A formation problem shows up in the daily loop, not in the strategy deck.

DCC is building an operating loop because informal coordination does not scale, and over-automation pretends judgment is a workflow step.

A structured inquiry is not the same as an automatic yes.

A quote is not a price scraped from a rate card with no one looking.

Production is not a button.

Review is not a notification.

The point of the loop is to make the path inspectable so that people can enter it, leave it, and continue it.

If only one person can see the whole path, DCC does not have an operating system. It has a founder with a very good memory.

## Founder dependency

Early institutions are founder-shaped. That is normal.

It becomes a risk when the public-facing infrastructure cannot survive a week of that person’s absence.

DCC is not claiming it has already escaped this. The opposite: this is a design problem the pilot has to face in public.

Related measures — founder-dependency ratio, system-transfer coverage, a failure and learning log — belong to institutional health. They are not artist-facing metrics, and they have no values here.

The hypothesis is simple:

**No critical organizational system should be understood by only one person.**

Until that is true, DCC should not talk about itself as finished infrastructure.

## Five artist infrastructure metrics

If DCC exists to make artist work more possible, then the measures that matter are not vanity counts.

They are questions about time, direction, friction, learning, and whether an experiment can continue.

The following is a **DCC proposal**. It is not an established standard in the field.

<EditorialFramework
  kind="proposal"
  title="Five Artist Infrastructure Metrics"
  intro="These measures ask whether infrastructure is actually reaching artists. They are methods, not current scores."
  status="Measurement begins with the DCC pilot. No values are published here."
>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="1. Idea-to-Experiment Time">
    **What it asks:** How long from a usable idea to a first real attempt on DCC infrastructure?

    **Methodology:** Count calendar days from a documented intake (inquiry, studio visit, or scoped request) to the first test that uses a DCC tool, space, or collaborator. Failed tests count. Unstarted ideas do not.

    **Status:** Measurement begins with the DCC pilot.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="2. Artist-Directed Resource Ratio">
    **What it asks:** Of the resources that moved, how much actually served artist-directed making?

    **Methodology:** For a project or period, separate artist-facing production (time, money, machine access, technical collaboration) from internal coordination and administration. Report the ratio, not a single heroic number.

    **Status:** Measurement begins with the DCC pilot.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="3. Administrative Friction per Project">
    **What it asks:** How much unpaid administrative labor did the artist have to perform to start?

    **Methodology:** Log the artist-facing steps that are not the work itself: forms, follow-ups, file-prep dead ends, waiting on a decision, re-explaining the project. Friction is counted even when DCC is the source of it.

    **Status:** Measurement begins with the DCC pilot.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="4. Capability Gain">
    **What it asks:** Did the artist leave with more ability to decide, not only with a finished object?

    **Methodology:** After a project or workshop, record what the artist can now do, ask, or refuse without DCC in the room. This is qualitative first. It should not be reduced to a satisfaction score.

    **Status:** Measurement begins with the DCC pilot.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="5. Experiment Continuation Rate">
    **What it asks:** Did the experiment continue — into a next test, a public presentation, or a documented pause — rather than dying at the first obstacle?

    **Methodology:** Follow a defined cohort of experiments across a stated window. Continuation includes a second attempt, a handoff, or an archived reason for stopping. Disappearance without record is a failure of infrastructure, not a neutral outcome.

    **Status:** Measurement begins with the DCC pilot.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
</EditorialFramework>

These metrics will be easy to fake later if DCC is not careful.

A short idea-to-experiment time can hide a tiny experiment.

A high continuation rate can hide work that only continues because the founder keeps catching it.

The methods have to stay visible with the numbers. That is why this essay publishes the methods first.

## Digital Institutional Literacy Standard

If technical judgment is an executive competency, then DCC should say what “enough” might mean — as a proposal, not as a certification product.

<EditorialFramework
  kind="proposal"
  title="Digital Institutional Literacy Standard"
  intro="Five levels of literacy inside a cultural organization. Most people do not need every level. The institution needs the levels to exist somewhere, with overlap, so the system can transfer."
  status="A DCC proposal. Not an accredited standard."
>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="Participant">
    Can use the tools the institution actually offers, ask for help without shame, and leave a trace someone else can follow (a file name, a note, a question).
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="Operator">
    Can run a recurring technical process without the founder: a workshop setup, a quote intake, a backup, a publication step, a fabrication handoff.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="Steward">
    Can keep a system alive across people: documentation, access, exceptions, and the judgment of when not to automate.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="Architect">
    Can design or substantially revise a system: what is owned, what is rented, what integrates, what is allowed to remain human.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="Executive" disclosure>
    Can govern technical decisions at the level of mission, budget, risk, and transfer. Proposed competencies:

    1. Systems thinking — seeing tools as interdependent, not as isolated purchases.
    2. Build vs. buy — choosing where judgment must stay in-house.
    3. Data ownership — knowing what the institution holds, where it lives, and who can leave with it.
    4. APIs and interoperability — understanding connection as a policy choice, not a vendor feature.
    5. Automation judgment — deciding what should never be automated.
    6. AI judgment — treating models as contingent labor, not as a department.
    7. Vendor literacy — reading a contract as an operating document.
    8. Technical debt — recognizing unpaid future work when it is being purchased.
    9. Measurement — refusing metrics that cannot show their method.
    10. Institutional transfer — planning for the organization to survive the people who currently understand it.

    These ten are a curriculum sketch. They are not a test DCC has administered.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
</EditorialFramework>

A small organization will not staff five job titles.

It still needs more than one brain holding the map.

## Equitable radical sharing, and where money actually goes

A growing DCC principle is **Equitable Radical Sharing**: institutional knowledge should compound outward while the people who produce that knowledge keep attribution, agency, and appropriate economic participation.

This is a proposal, not a legal policy.

In practice it suggests three visibility levels.

**Public by default:** diagrams, methodologies, templates, operating frameworks, lessons, failure reports, measurement systems.

**Shared with context:** specialized fabrication procedures, estimator logic, curriculum, contributor-created knowledge, safety-sensitive material.

**Private by necessity:** personal data, credentials, payments, security-sensitive information, confidential agreements, private communications.

The Journal is the public layer of that stack. It is not the place to dump a password or a private artist file in the name of openness.

Beside sharing sits a capital question.

DCC’s working ladder — again a proposal — is:

1. Keep the lights on.
2. Pay the people who already make the institution real.
3. Reduce founder subsidy.
4. Fund artist-directed experiments.
5. Only then buy more tools.

A new platform that arrives before step two is not infrastructure. It is a way to avoid payroll.

Community infrastructure return, in this frame, is not a social-impact multiplier. It is a plainer test:

Did the money create capacity that other people can use after the invoice is paid?

If the answer is no, DCC should not call the spend a public good.

## What this essay is not

It is not a completed cost study.

It is not a comparison shopping list for museum software.

It is not live Airtable.

It is not a claim that DCC already operates without founder dependency.

Those would be comforting, and false.

This is a living essay. The methods are being published before the numbers so that, when numbers arrive, they have somewhere honest to live.

The work now is operational: run the loop, write the receipt, teach the literacy, and refuse to fill empty cells with invented proof.
