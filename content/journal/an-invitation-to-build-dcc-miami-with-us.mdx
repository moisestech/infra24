I want to invite more people into something we're still learning how to build.

It's called DCC Miami.

Fabiola and I have been bringing artists, makers, designers, creative technologists, educators, fabricators, and other curious people into conversation. Some work with 3D printers and code. Others work with sound, moving image, installation, electronics, performance, or materials that don't belong neatly to any discipline.

This is not an invitation to join an organization that has figured everything out.

It is an invitation to help shape one.

## Start with what you're trying to do

A useful community does not begin with everyone giving an impressive introduction.

Sometimes it begins with a smaller sentence.

*I'm trying to make this, and I don't know how.*

Or: *I know how to do this, and I'd like to help.*

Or: *I've been experimenting with something strange. Does anyone else care about it?*

I like the possibility that those sentences could lead somewhere in Miami: a studio visit, a shared experiment, an introduction, a workshop, a small paid commission, or simply a new friendship.

Not every conversation needs to turn into a project. Not everyone needs to become a client. Some people will want to participate quietly and learn from what others share.

All of that counts.

## What we're trying to make possible

Miami has cultural institutions, universities, individual studios, fabrication shops, and people who know astonishingly specific things.

But knowing that these things exist is different from knowing how to reach them.

Someone wants to make a physical object from a digital sketch. Someone else has spent years learning how to finish prints so they stop looking like prints. An artist working with projection needs to talk to someone who understands mapping. A performer wants to experiment with interactive sound but doesn't know what equipment is necessary.

Sometimes the missing element isn't a new tool. It's a conversation with the right person.

I don't think every artist should learn every technology. Nor should DCC own every machine, lead every workshop, or become the gatekeeper for every introduction.

The more interesting possibility is a network in which people become easier to find, learn from, collaborate with, and hire—on terms they actually agree to.

## Come with a question, not a résumé

If you make things, tell us about something you're working on, even if it isn't finished.

If you know a process, share one thing people often misunderstand about it.

If you have access to equipment, tell us what it can and cannot do. That doesn't mean you are volunteering it to strangers.

If you run a studio, lab, or cultural program, maybe there's a small experiment worth trying together.

And if you're curious but unfamiliar with these tools, that curiosity belongs here too.

I'd rather someone say *I don't know much about this, but I want to learn* than feel obliged to perform expertise they don't have.

## What kind of community?

The word *network* can sound transactional: one person has an opportunity and another wants it.

That's part of real life, but it's much too small a description.

A creative community needs room for friendship, critique, generosity, and experiments that fail. It should respect the time of people who teach as well as those who are still learning.

It also needs boundaries.

Joining a conversation doesn't mean your work should be reposted without asking. Being good at something doesn't mean you're always available. An introduction should be welcome on both sides. Paid production deserves an actual scope, fair terms, and clear responsibilities.

A small community can still be an extractive one. Size alone tells us almost nothing about whether people feel welcome, useful, or respected.

I hope we can make DCC a place people want to return to, rather than another group they have to keep up with.

## Learn in public, protect in private

The experiments are still small. We have more questions than answers.

Did somebody learn a process? Did a question find the right person? Was a collaboration enjoyable and fair? Did an object get finished? Did someone gain confidence, even though no money changed hands?

Those are things worth documenting when the people involved want to share them.

That is different from harvesting a group's private conversations or treating a member's name as a commercial lead.

If this becomes useful, the evidence should come from real projects, actual participation, and people who chose to be represented.

The public journal is one place where we can think through that work, including what doesn't work.

## An invitation

Tell us what you're making, what you're curious about, or what kind of problem you like helping people solve.

You don't need a perfect idea, a machine, or a credential. You can begin with a question. You can begin by listening. You can begin by showing us something unfinished.

You can also tell us when you think we're getting something wrong.

That's part of building it together.

You can find the project at [dcc.miami](https://www.dcc.miami/).

We're trying to create the conditions for more people in Miami to make work they haven't yet had the opportunity to make.

It will be more interesting if we don't try to do that alone.
