A bronze sculpture can corrode.

A mural can fade.

A fountain can clog.

Cities already know these problems. They have departments, contractors, and habits for them.

Then the work includes a computer.

Someone has to own the machine.

Someone has to know the password.

Someone has to pay for the network.

Someone has to decide what happens when the vendor disappears.

Public digital art can make a city feel awake. It can also leave that city responsible for a technological system nobody remembers how to operate.

<EditorialPullQuote>
  The city has acquired a small technological system.
</EditorialPullQuote>

This is not an argument against digital public art.

It is an argument that **ambitious digital public art requires corresponding civic technical capacity.**

Public digital art exposes a gap between cultural commissioning and civic infrastructure.

When a municipality commissions a technologically complex public artwork, it may inherit hardware, software, networks, cloud services, licenses, credentials, APIs, sensors, data, operating systems, cybersecurity obligations, replacement components, documentation, ongoing operating costs, and eventually a migration.

The city is no longer only a patron.

It becomes some combination of owner, operator, maintainer, archivist, technology stakeholder, conservator, and — years later — migration team.

## Public art has always been infrastructure

American public-art programs did not begin with LEDs.

Percent-for-art policies set aside a share of capital construction budgets to commission or acquire artwork for public places. The details vary by city and state. Americans for the Arts compiles municipal ordinance samples and notes programs on the books as early as 1959 in Philadelphia.<Cite id="afta-percent" /> That dating comes from that compilation, not from a rereading of Philadelphia’s original ordinance here.

At the state level, the National Assembly of State Arts Agencies describes typical set-asides as about one percent of construction or renovation costs, used for commission, purchase, and sometimes maintenance. It also states the obvious that practitioners already know: programs are not identical.<Cite id="nasaa-percent" />

The federal Art in Architecture program is more specific. GSA reserves one-half of one percent of estimated construction cost for qualifying new federal buildings and modernizations, as a commission for site-specific artwork.<Cite id="gsa-aia" />

Miami-Dade’s Art in Public Places program is one of the early county programs in the United States. The Department of Cultural Affairs states that it was established in 1973 with an ordinance allocating 1.5 percent of construction cost of new county buildings for the purchase or commission of artworks.<Cite id="mdc-app" />

The Art in Public Places Trust’s published responsibilities include selection, **maintenance**, planning, public education, and curating of works acquired by the program.<Cite id="mdc-trust" />

That last word matters.

Long-term civic ownership was already part of the public-art idea.

What changed is the kind of object the city might now be asked to keep alive.

## From monument to media system

Artists have used existing technological infrastructure as a public canvas for a long time.

Public Art Fund’s *Messages to the Public* (1982–1990) commissioned artist projects specifically for the Spectacolor board in Times Square — noncommercial animations inserted into a loop of commercial messages.<Cite id="paf-messages" />

Times Square Arts still programs that kind of borrowed infrastructure. *Midnight Moment* presents synchronized artist works on the district’s electronic billboards for three minutes each night. The program’s own page calls it the world’s largest and longest-running digital public art exhibition; that ranking is the presenter’s language, not an independent audit.<Cite id="tsq-midnight" />

The useful distinction is not “old media versus new media.”

It is this:

**Programming someone else’s existing technological system is different from asking a city to acquire and keep a bespoke permanent technological system.**

The first case can still fail. A board can go dark. A vendor can change the format.

The second case transfers a stack.

## The city is the client

Once the artwork contains computers, the commissioning relationship changes.

Who owns the computer?

Who owns the source code?

Who owns the cloud accounts?

Where are credentials stored, and in whose email domain?

Who pays for connectivity?

Can the work function if the network dies?

What happens when an API changes?

Who is allowed to reboot it at 2 a.m.?

Can a display be replaced without the artist’s approval?

What happens when the original vendor is gone?

What happens when procurement cannot buy a discontinued controller?

<EditorialPullQuote>
  These are not edge cases. They are the artwork.
</EditorialPullQuote>

A bronze plaque does not have a certificate to renew.

A mural does not have a dependency list.

A fountain does not phone home.

Digital public art can do all of those things, and still look, on opening night, like a sculpture that happens to glow.

## Maintenance is part of the medium

Museum conservation already has language for this problem.

The Smithsonian Time-based Media & Digital Art working group exists because film, video, software, web, performance, and installation works present specific challenges for conservation, documentation, installation, and acquisition. The works have duration. Their components become obsolete.<Cite id="si-tbma" />

Hirshhorn conservators in that same Smithsonian conversation note that migration can be an acceptable preservation method and that it also risks losing qualities tied to the original technology.

The Guggenheim’s Variable Media Initiative, and the Variable Media Network around it, asked a sharper question: how might a work outlast the medium it was born in? The strategy is to describe behaviors independently enough that storage, migration, emulation, or reinterpretation can be planned before the original format dies.<Cite id="guggenheim-vmi" /><Cite id="variablemedia-net" />

<EditorialPullQuote>
  Digital conservation is not only the preservation of objects. It is the preservation of behavior.
</EditorialPullQuote>

That sentence is a DCC compression of that conservation literature, not a quotation from a single manual.

For a city, the implication is practical.

If the identity of the work is a behavior — a rhythm of light, a response to weather, a latency, a scale of pixel — then replacing a dead screen is not janitorial work.

It is conservation.

It should be named that way in the contract, before anyone needs it.

## Case study: The Bay Lights

Leo Villareal’s *The Bay Lights* is useful here because it is not a cautionary fable invented to scold cities. It is a public artwork on a working bridge, produced by the nonprofit Illuminate, that had to meet weather as a medium.

Illuminate’s project record places the debut on 5 March 2013, originally 25,000 individually controllable LEDs along 1.8 miles of the western span of the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge. A temporary installation came down and returned. On the tenth anniversary the lights went dark while a new campaign began.<Cite id="illuminate-bay" />

Illuminate later described the 2026 return as a **complete rebuild, not a repair**: a custom-engineered LED system designed for wind load, salt air, vibration, and long-term reliability, with 48,000 LEDs.<Cite id="illuminate-rebuild" />

The point is not spectacle.

The point is that **the environment becomes part of the medium**, and so does the engineering required to stay in that environment.

A Miami commission that ignores heat, humidity, salt, storm, and brightness is not being site-specific.

It is being optimistic.

## Permanence may be the wrong goal

Public art programs often speak the language of permanence because capital budgets do.

Variable media conservation speaks the language of identity: what must remain true for this to remain the same artwork?

What must stay identical?

What may change?

What may be emulated?

Can hardware be substituted?

Can software be rewritten if the behavior holds?

Does the particular screen matter sculpturally?

Is latency part of the work?

Is the exact sensor important, or is the sensing important?

Those questions are not an excuse for careless replacement.

They are how you avoid two failure modes: a dead original, and a lively fake.

A city that cannot answer them will freeze the first box it bought until the box fails, then guess.

## Sometimes the best network is no network

Connectivity can give a work live data, remote control, and the feeling of being of the moment.

It can also add attack surface, vendor dependency, extra failure modes, privacy risk, and a bill that never closes.

This is not a rule that interactive work should be offline.

It is a commissioning question:

**What does the network actually do for the public experience, and what happens when it is gone?**

If the work cannot fail into a local state, the artwork includes the internet.

Say so.

Pay for so.

Document so.

## Digital Public Art Technical Rider

The following is a **DCC proposal**.

It is not a municipal ordinance, not a conservation standard, and not a form you can submit.

It is a commissioning framework for technologically mediated public artwork: the questions a city should be able to answer before it accepts the keys.

<EditorialFramework
  kind="proposal"
  title="Digital Public Art Technical Rider"
  intro="Eleven sections a city and an artist can complete together. The rider is a document of identity, inventory, and transfer — not a dashboard."

  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="01 — Artistic invariants" disclosure>
    Document essential behaviors, the artist-defined identity of the work, acceptable substitutions, prohibited changes, and a migration philosophy.

    Question: what must remain true for this to remain the same artwork?
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="02 — Hardware inventory" disclosure>
    Document computers, displays, projectors, LEDs, controllers, sensors, network equipment, power systems, enclosures, custom electronics, and spare parts.

    Include manufacturer, model, serial where appropriate, expected replacement cycle, and approved alternatives.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="03 — Software inventory" disclosure>
    Document source code, operating system, application versions, frameworks, libraries, codecs, dependencies, build instructions, runtime requirements, and licenses.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="04 — Network and data" disclosure>
    Document internet requirements, ports and protocols, APIs, certificates, domains, data sources, offline behavior, local fallback, and privacy or security requirements.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="05 — Accounts and credentials" disclosure>
    Document account ownership, city-controlled email, credential storage, MFA, recovery procedure, and vendor permissions.

    Never publish real credentials. The Journal will not.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="06 — Monitoring and restart" disclosure>
    Document the normal operating state, monitoring, alert behavior, reboot procedure, recovery sequence, public-facing failure state, and escalation contacts.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="07 — Security" disclosure>
    Document attack surface, remote access, patch responsibility, privacy implications of cameras or sensors, network segmentation where applicable, and incident-response responsibility.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="08 — Maintenance and spare parts" disclosure>
    Document routine maintenance, cleaning, consumables, spares, recommended replacement stock, inspection schedule, and technician requirements.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="09 — Ten-year operating model" disclosure>
    Require estimates for power, connectivity, subscriptions, cloud costs, software licenses, replacement hardware, inspection, specialist labor, consumables, and likely migration events.

    This article does not fabricate those numbers. The rider asks the commission to produce them.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="10 — Archival package" disclosure>
    Document source code, a compiled or runtime version, media, configuration, diagrams, manuals, an artist interview, photographs or video, and version history.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
  <EditorialFrameworkItem title="11 — End of life" disclosure>
    Define when operation becomes impractical, artist consultation, migration criteria, deinstallation, archival capture, storage or disposition of hardware, and preservation of software and media.
  </EditorialFrameworkItem>
</EditorialFramework>

A city that cannot fill most of these sections does not yet have a public artwork.

It has a vendor relationship with a sculpture attached.

## Public art can become civic capacity

A city should not have to invent a technology stack for every commission.

Reusable municipal infrastructure might include secure media players, outdoor enclosures, power and data connection standards, approved sensor interfaces, network access patterns, documentation templates, software archival procedures, spare-part practices, technical artist rosters, conservation expertise, and shared display infrastructure.

The question is not how to install more screens.

**What technical infrastructure would allow artists to use computation in public space without every commission having to invent and maintain an entire technology stack from scratch?**

That is a civic-capacity question. It rhymes with DCC’s institutional question — owning technical decisions rather than renting them — without turning public art into a DCC product.

## Miami, specifically

Miami already has a public-art program with capital set-aside, a Trust, and a published maintenance responsibility.<Cite id="mdc-app" /><Cite id="mdc-trust" />

What this essay has **not** verified is a digital-specific operating protocol: who holds credentials, how ten-year costs are estimated, how hurricane downtime is specified, or how software migration is commissioned.<Cite id="mdc-digital-maintenance" />

Until that protocol is public, DCC should not pretend Miami has solved the stack.

The local conditions are not theoretical.

Heat.

Humidity.

Salt air.

Hurricanes.

Flooding where the site is exposed.

Outdoor brightness.

Power interruptions.

Public-space maintenance that already struggles with analog objects.

A digital-public-art strategy for Miami should not begin with “How do we install more screens?”

It should begin with:

**What technical infrastructure would let artists use computation in public space sustainably?**

## The city should not buy a black box

A successful technological commission should leave the municipality able to understand:

what it owns,

how it works,

how to restart it,

who maintains it,

what may be replaced,

where documentation lives,

what data exists,

which vendor dependencies exist,

and when migration is necessary.

The city does not need to manufacture every tool it uses.

**It needs to own its technical decisions.**

That is the same literacy argument the Journal makes for cultural organizations, applied to a public client.

Opening night is the easiest moment in the life of a digital artwork.

Everyone who built it is still there.

Everything is new.

The passwords are known.

The difficult artwork begins years later.
