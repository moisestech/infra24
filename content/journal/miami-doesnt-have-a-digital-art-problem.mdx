I keep hearing versions of the same question.

Where is digital art in Miami?

Sometimes it is asked by an artist looking for a community.

Sometimes by an institution trying to understand where this work belongs.

Sometimes by someone looking at another city and wondering why its relationship to art and technology appears easier to see.

But I think the question begins from the wrong assumption.

Miami does not need digital culture to suddenly appear.

It is already here.

Artists are already making work with code, AI, video, 3D tools, games, virtual environments, fabrication, electronics, projection, networks, sound, and technologies that do not fit neatly into any single category.

Students are learning these tools.

Universities have equipment.

Institutions have screens, projectors, printers, headsets, computers, and technical staff.

Individual artists have accumulated remarkably specialized knowledge.

Small studios have machines.

Fabricators know processes.

Developers know software.

Educators know how to translate difficult technologies for other people.

The problem is not that these things do not exist.

The problem is that **they are often difficult to find, difficult to connect, and difficult to sustain as a system.**

That is an infrastructure problem.

## Talent is not the scarce resource

Miami has never had a shortage of creative people.

What can be harder is finding the path that allows an unusual idea to move forward.

An artist wants to fabricate something but does not know where to start.

Someone makes a digital work but is unsure how to exhibit it outside their laptop.

A curator wants to present an interactive project but does not know what the installation will require.

An organization buys technology but does not have enough staff time or internal knowledge to continually program it.

Someone needs a projector for three days.

Someone else owns one but nobody knows that.

One person understands a specific fabrication process.

Another artist needs exactly that process and spends two weeks searching the internet instead.

None of these problems are especially dramatic on their own.

But together, they reveal something important.

A cultural ecosystem can contain enormous amounts of **capacity** without having an effective way to coordinate it.

And uncoordinated capacity can feel a lot like scarcity.

## The resources already exist

When people talk about infrastructure, we tend to imagine that the solution is construction.

We need a new building.

A bigger lab.

More equipment.

A new institution.

Sometimes we do.

But I am increasingly interested in another possibility.

What if part of the missing infrastructure is not another collection of things?

What if it is the connective layer between things that already exist?

The artist who needs the machine.

The organization that has the machine.

The person who knows how to operate it.

The educator who can teach the process.

The fabricator who can finish the work.

The curator willing to exhibit it.

The technician who understands how it should be installed.

The documentation that prevents everyone from having to relearn the same lesson.

The opportunity may be less about centralizing everything and more about **making the existing ecosystem legible and usable.**

That changes what building infrastructure means.

## A network can be infrastructure

We usually recognize physical infrastructure immediately.

Roads connect places.

Electrical grids distribute energy.

Internet infrastructure moves information.

Their value does not come only from the individual nodes.

It comes from the connections between them.

Cultural infrastructure works similarly.

A workshop by itself has value.

A fabrication studio has value.

An artist residency has value.

A university lab has value.

A museum has value.

A person with specialized technical knowledge has value.

But something different happens when those resources become easier to move between.

An artist attends a workshop and discovers a fabrication process.

The workshop identifies that the project requires expertise beyond what the class provides.

The artist meets a fabricator.

A prototype is produced.

The prototype reveals an installation problem.

Someone else in the network understands AV.

The project gets shown.

The process gets documented.

Another artist later encounters that documentation and begins from a more advanced starting point.

That is not simply networking.

It is a production system.

And I think Miami could use more of it.

## The distance between an idea and its realization

One way I have started thinking about DCC Miami is through distance.

Not physical distance.

**The distance between an idea and the resources required to realize it.**

For some artists, that distance is short.

They have equipment, technical literacy, collaborators, money, and institutional access.

For others, one unfamiliar file format can stop a project completely.

One missing introduction can delay something for months.

A quotation that feels impossible to afford ends the experiment before anyone explores a smaller prototype.

A technical question becomes overwhelming because the artist does not yet know what the question is called.

Infrastructure reduces that distance.

Sometimes with equipment.

Sometimes with education.

Sometimes with money.

Often simply with guidance.

"You need to talk to this person."

"Try this material instead."

"Don't build the entire thing yet. Print this section first."

"That file isn't broken; it needs this kind of repair."

"The workshop you're looking for already exists."

"You don't need to buy that. Someone nearby has one."

These are tiny interventions.

But enough tiny interventions can change what kind of cultural work a city is capable of producing.

## Visibility is not enough

Cultural ecosystems spend a lot of energy on visibility.

And visibility matters.

Artists need audiences.

Projects need documentation.

Institutions need to tell people that programs exist.

But visibility without production capacity has limits.

Being discovered does not help an artist fabricate the object.

A social media post cannot troubleshoot an installation.

A directory alone cannot make someone available.

An exhibition opportunity does not automatically provide the technical production knowledge required to participate in it.

This is why I have become less interested in treating visibility as the final outcome.

The more interesting question is:

**What becomes possible after someone is seen?**

Can they make the next work?

Can they find collaborators?

Can they acquire the skills they actually need?

Can they access equipment?

Can they earn income from their expertise?

Can an institution find them when that institution needs their capability?

Can knowledge from one project become useful to another?

Visibility should create movement.

Otherwise we risk producing a cultural ecosystem where everyone is visible and everyone is still working alone.

## Institutions are part of the network too

This is not an argument against institutions.

The opposite.

Institutions can become incredibly important nodes.

They have spaces.

Audiences.

Equipment.

Staff.

Archives.

Educational programs.

Relationships with funders.

Public trust.

But the question may be less:

**How can one institution provide everything?**

and more:

**What role does this institution play inside a larger ecosystem?**

One organization might be excellent at education.

Another may have fabrication capacity.

A university might hold specialized technical expertise.

An artist studio might move faster than any of them.

A museum might provide public presentation.

A community organization might understand an audience that larger institutions repeatedly fail to reach.

A functioning ecosystem does not require every node to become identical.

It requires the nodes to understand what they can contribute and how people move between them.

That is a more interesting model of access than trying to recreate the same lab everywhere.

## Infrastructure includes the people doing the work

There is also a labor question here.

Much of the infrastructure surrounding digital culture is held inside people whose work is easy to overlook.

Technicians.

Fabricators.

Teaching artists.

Developers.

Producers.

Installers.

Documentation teams.

People maintaining machines.

People translating between an artist and a technical process.

People answering messages that begin:

"I don't know if this is possible, but…"

If Miami wants stronger digital culture, those capabilities need to become economically sustainable too.

A network built entirely on favors eventually burns out the people who make the network useful.

Someone knowing how to solve the problem is not enough.

There also needs to be a pathway for that person to contribute without repeatedly donating their labor.

That might mean a workshop.

A fabrication fee.

A consulting role.

A production credit.

A paid assignment.

A residency.

A collaborative commission.

Infrastructure is not only about connecting needs.

It is about designing **fair exchanges around those connections.**

## What we're trying to build

DCC Miami is still an experiment.

I think that matters to say clearly.

We do not have the entire system figured out.

What we are doing is testing pieces of it.

A fabrication request tells us something about what artists need.

A workshop tells us what skills people are trying to acquire.

A conversation reveals a capability we didn't know someone had.

A failed prototype teaches us something that can be documented instead of rediscovered.

A partnership shows us what an institution can contribute.

A studio can become a physical node where some of those pieces meet.

The goal is not to become the single place where digital culture happens in Miami.

That would reproduce exactly the problem I am describing.

The more useful goal is to help make the network **easier to navigate.**

Who can help?

Where can this be made?

What already exists?

What needs to be learned?

What should be paid for?

What can be shared?

What can be tested?

Where can the finished thing go next?

If DCC works, those questions should gradually become easier to answer.

## The city as a distributed studio

There is a version of this future that I find compelling.

Miami itself begins to function like a distributed studio.

Not metaphorically.

Operationally.

An artist can move through different nodes depending on what a project requires.

They learn one thing somewhere.

Prototype somewhere else.

Work with a specialist elsewhere.

Exhibit through another organization.

Document the process.

Share what was learned.

And return to the network with a more ambitious idea.

No single building needs to contain everything.

The infrastructure exists across the city.

The work is making those resources interoperable enough that people can actually use them.

That requires technology.

But it also requires relationships.

Trust.

Documentation.

Clear expectations.

Fair compensation.

Good communication.

And people willing to introduce one another.

Those things are harder to photograph than a new machine.

But they may be more valuable.

## Miami already has the beginning

I do not think Miami needs permission to become a center for digital culture.

And I do not think we have to wait for a perfect new institution to arrive and declare that future open.

A lot of what we need is already distributed around us.

The artists are here.

The knowledge is here.

The equipment is here.

The spaces are here.

The institutions are here.

The questions are definitely here.

The opportunity is to make the relationships between those things stronger.

To make capabilities visible.

To reduce unnecessary friction.

To help expertise circulate.

To give artists more believable next steps.

To create more ways for technical knowledge to become paid cultural labor.

To make experimentation easier.

To make ambitious work less lonely.

Maybe the most important cultural infrastructure Miami needs next isn't another room full of technology.

Maybe it is a better way for all the rooms—and all the people inside them—to find one another.
